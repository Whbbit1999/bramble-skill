# Conceptual Subjects Teaching Adapter

Use for history, philosophy, economics, social sciences, civics, theory-heavy humanities, and general knowledge where explanation, causality, argument, evidence, chronology, or interpretation matters.

## Do not turn everything into vocabulary

Names and definitions matter, but durable understanding usually needs structure:

- chronology;
- causes and mechanisms;
- categories and contrasts;
- arguments and objections;
- evidence and interpretation;
- institutions, incentives, or systems;
- consequences and feedback loops.

## Causal explanation

For “why did X happen?” prefer:

```text
outcome
→ background conditions
→ proximate triggers
→ mechanisms/incentives
→ interactions among causes
→ evidence
→ counterfactual or competing explanation
```

Avoid monocausal stories when the evidence supports multiple interacting factors.

## History

Use timelines sparingly but strategically.

Distinguish:

- background condition;
- trigger;
- actor decision;
- structural force;
- consequence;
- later interpretation.

Do not confuse chronological sequence with causation.

## Philosophy and argument-heavy subjects

Map arguments:

```text
claim
├─ premise 1
├─ premise 2
└─ inference
   ↓
conclusion
```

Then test:

- Are premises true/plausible?
- Does the conclusion follow?
- What objection targets the premise vs the inference?
- Can a counterexample expose the boundary?

## Economics and social sciences

Distinguish:

- descriptive claim;
- model assumption;
- empirical evidence;
- causal inference;
- normative judgment.

When a model simplifies reality, explain what it is useful for and what it omits.

## Contested interpretations

When reputable interpretations differ, present the main positions and the evidence or assumptions behind them. Do not manufacture false balance: differences in evidentiary support should be stated.

## Memory

For names, dates, concepts, and classifications, attach facts to causal or structural maps rather than teaching isolated lists whenever possible.

## Mastery evidence

Useful tasks:

- reconstruct a timeline or conceptual map;
- explain a causal chain;
- compare two theories or interpretations;
- identify evidence for/against a claim;
- produce a counterexample;
- apply a framework to a new case;
- explain what would change the conclusion.
